I. William Gibson 
1. 1948
A. Born March 17 in Conway, South Carolina.
2. 1966
A. Following the death of his mother, and having experienced the death of his father previous when he was a young child, Gibson begins to travel in Calfornia and Europe to immerse himself in the counterculture of the late 1960s. 
3. 1967
A. Relocates to Canada to avoid the draft.  Starts a community in Toronto to shelter other draft dodgers.
4. 1972
A. Enrolls at the University of British Columbia to study literature.  
i. It is at UBC that Gibson is first encouraged to begin writing science fiction.  
5. 1977
A. Publishes his first story, "Fragments of a Hologram Rose," which already looks ahead to themes of cyberpunk, cyberculture, and dystopia in his writing.
6. 1984
A. Publishes his first novel, Neuromancer.
B. After his viewing of Blade Runner in 1982, Gibson rewrote portions of Neuromancer up to twelve times fearing that readers would assume he had simply ripped off Ridley Scott's film. 
i. Though comparisons to Blade Runner are inevitable, Neuromancer is prasied for its creativty and originality and established Gibson as a founding influence in cyberculture who influcence in still felt contemporarily across creative genres; for example, The Matrix Trilogy.   
ii.  For example,  Lawrence Person in his "Notes Toward a Postcyberpunk Manifesto" (1998), identifies Neuromancer as "the archetypal cyberpunk work".  Larry McCaffery describes the concept of the matrix in Neuromancer as a place where "data dance with human consciousness ... human memory is literalized and mechanized ... multi-national information systems mutate and breed into startling new structures whose beauty and complexity are unimaginable, mystical, and above all nonhuman."  In 2005, as part of the Time list of the 100 best English-language novels written since 1923, Lev Grossman stated of Neuromancer: "There is no way to overstate how radical Gibson's first and best novel was when it first appeared."
II. Neuromancer (1984)
1. Themes.
A. Cyberpunk--Explores the effects of technology, cybernetics, and computer networks on humans. 
B. Cyberspace--Coined by Gibson in 1982 as widespread, interconnected digital technology.
C. Dystopia--A society characterized by oppression, poverty, violence, sickness, war, and so on.  Much of Dick's work is set in near-future society's where despite the presence of advanced technology, characters live in run-down locations such as the Sprawl in Neuromancer. 
III. Points to consider for discussion:
1. Think about how the Sprawl expresses the concept of Cyberpunk.  For example, the Sprawl is a run-down, dystopian setting and yet in contains access to advanced technology.   
2. Consider how Cyberspace functions in today's reading.   For example, in many ways Cyberspace is much more real than--as well as a means of escape from--the "real" world for characters such as Case.
3. Building on the previous point, think in more detail about why Cyberspace is so important to Case.  Think also about how Gibson characterizes Case's relationship with Linda Lee.
4. Gibson charaterizes Molly as a very strong character.  In relation to her character, we see Gibson continuing to draw attention to the theme of Cyberpunk, for example, in her cybernetic enhancements.  Despite her strength and her ability to navigate the world of the Sprawl, the Sprawl is still a dystopia.   With this in mind, just as Molly dominates Case in many moments in the plot, she herself remains a servant to more powerful forces.  
April 14 - April 16 NotesApril 14 - April 16 Notes
I. Points to consider for discussion:  
1. Begin to think about how Gibson characterizes the A.I. called "Wintermute."   Should we see Wintermute as a threat in Gibson's portrayal?  For example, Wintermute essentially controls Armitage who is in charge of Molly and Case.  In a sense, Wintermute has the ability to determine the reality and freedom of human characters even though it is not human, at least in a biological sense.  In Chapter Twelve, Michele characterizes Wintermute as a "demon."  At the same time, Wintermute has awareness of itself, for example, when it states that it is one part of a greater potential "entity" in Chapter Nine.  Wintermute also appears to Case in "human form" in Cyberspace.  Though not biologically human, we might consider in terms of characterization if Wintermute does not have human qualities and drives.   
2. Moreover, building upon the previous point, Wintermute is the product of industry.  How does industry, as protrayed by Gibson, drive the Cyberpunk overlap of humans and technology?  
3. The introduction of Zion might remind some of you--along with other references in Gibson's setting and plot-- of the 1999 film, The Matrix. 
A. The culture of Zion on the fringes of society again postions Gibson historically as an advocate of countercultural ideology.  The Zionites, to a degree, stand in opposition to the concept of industry mentioned above.   
B. The setting in which the Zionites live, and their culture, continues to show the merging of technology with human concerns, but here, specifically, we see an overlap between technology and religion.  For example, in addition to ganja, the Zionites use electronic music as a form of worship.   
C. Zion engages the concept of "Afrofuturism" defined as a cultural movement and artistic aesthetic that uses science fiction, technology, and other futuristic elements to explore Black history and culture.  Specifically, Afrofuturism aims at empowerment in terms of connecting Black people from the diaspora with their African ancestry and to envision a future free of hierarchies based on race, gender, class, or sexuality.  For example, even though colonialism would be a part of the ancestry of the Zionites, they have forged their own home and a shared, empowered past in the setting of Zion.    
i. The term is coined in 1993 by Mark Dery, but Afrofuturist trends have been around since the early 19th century.

I. Victor LaValle
1. 1972
A. Born February 3 in Queens, New York.
2. 1999
A. After attending Cornell and Columbia University, publishes his first collection of stories, Slapboxing with Jesus.
i. Focuses on the lives of Black and Latinx men in New York City in the 1970s and '80s.
3. 2002
A. Publishes his first novel, The Ecstatic, based upon one of the characters in Slapboxing with Jesus.
4. 2016
A. Publishes the novella, Black Tom.
i. Like his previous work, Black Tom, is published to great acclaim. 
ii. While LaValle's previous focus had blurred the boundaries of reality with characters who are the survivors or who have been committed to a mental institution, Black Tom operates much more centrally as a work of speculative fiction which responds to H. P. Lovecraft's "The Horror at Red Hook."  Specifically, LaValle responds to the racism of Lovecraft's writing. 
5. 2017
A. Publishes both The Changling (a novel) and Destroyer (a graphic novel)
i. Both works represent works of speculative fiction and, with Destroyer, we continue to see LaValle responding to previous authors.  For example, Destoyer uses Mary Shelley's work to explore contemporary racial and environment issues.
II. Destroyer (2017) 
1. Written by Victor LaValle and illustrated by Dietrich Smith.
2. Points to consider for discussion:
A. Consider Smith's visual representation of Victor's Creature.  Shelley provides us with little physical description of the Creature following the moment of his initial animation.  This leaves the Creature's appearance greatly to the imagination of the reader.  How does Smith's design of the Creature compare to what you imagined when reading Shelley's novel?
B. In several instances in the first three chapters of LaValle's graphic novel, the Creature's violence functions as a mode of social or environmental justice that nevertheless harms others, in some cases the very people (or creatures) who would be helped.  This results from the Creature's being foremost driven by anger.  Anger is an understandable emotion in the novel, but consider if LaValle suggests that this emotion, though understandable, makes one as desctructive as those who are themselves "destroyers."
C. Compare Dr. Baker's bringing Akai back to life via nanotech to Victor's creation of the creature.  Dr. Baker herself, in Chapter Three, compares herself to Victor in stating that perhaps Victor was a man motivated by grief as opposed to a "mad scientist."  As you think about this, extend your consideration to the way in which Victor's abandonment of his Creature compares to Dr. Baker's "educating" Akai following his resurrection. 
i. Dr. Baker is named after Josephine Baker, a dancer, singer, and actress; Baker used her celebrity status to call attention to racial discrimination in the United States and Europe. She also helped to impact the civil rights movement in the US.
